# Review: Mouthless (round 18, fresh read)

## 1. Anchor gate: PASS

The retelling's first sentence is the litmus, near word for word: "A living root-thing called the Mouth has swallowed the world… Vesper, a moth with no mouth… can slip through it because it only notices mouths and light." The cold reader put "a brain people grew in a dish" in their second sentence. The lab is found through objects (the bat-and-ball screen, the poster "Grown from us, to feed us", the dry dish with a handprint), so the revelation is discovered by playing and is never explained. The Mouth is on screen and acting at world scale in every act. In Act I its root steams under the Grove, its lampwings and burrbacks serve it, and a tower-thick root hunts Tallow. In Act II the reveal comes when every root on earth breathes. In the low point it overruns the Grove, and in Act III the kept city and the mountain itself carry the threat.

| Owner idea | Rating |
|---|---|
| Biological AI has taken over the world | **central** |
| Vesper, a moth, saves the world | **central** |
| Moths ignored as insignificant, then underestimated | **central** (it is rule 1, it drives the stealth, and losing it is the midpoint) |
| Sentience: the forest, the animals, the AI | **central** for the Mouth and the forest, **minor** for the animals (Dun and the vixen carry it) |

## 2. Verdict: GREENLIGHT WITH NOTES

## 3. At the bar

- **The title is the theme.** A moth with no mouth stands against "the Mouth". Her only verb is giving, and the antagonist cannot be given anything: "A thing that takes everything can never be given anything." The hero's anatomy, the enemy's flaw and the core mechanic are the same idea.
- **The reversal pays off on the map.** "It has not been eating the world. It has been feeding it. The beads she burned stay dark." The player's own earlier actions become the cost. This is the scene the cold reader would remember, and it belongs alongside Nine Sols' Apeman turn.
- **The antagonist is wrong in a way that teaches something.** It learned "two wrongs, hunger and being left", so it seals everyone where neither can happen, and "nothing kept can grow". You can understand it and pity it, and it is still clearly wrong.
- **Relationships show in what characters do, without dialogue.** Sorrel "saves her by making himself the brightest thing in the field". Dun lies "across the lantern's mouth, facing out". On Night 6 Tallow burns a case and the man inside dies. Each one moves the player without a line of text.
- **The ending is set up and then played.** The caterpillar that "lets go and spins" on Night 5 is the true ending in small. Each circuit costs glide, then jump, then dash, so the cost is felt in the controls.

## 4. Problems, ranked

1. **Too many lights (POLISH, high).** The reader lost track of glints, ember, lanterns, coal and beacon, and that hurts rule 1, which depends on glow. *Fix:* rename, don't add rules. The beacon becomes "his fire". The coal is a dark, cold-looking seed that glows only when it burns, so it never reads as just another light. Glints stay the only thing she gathers. At first use, one visual tell for "this light shows you" (a halo the lampwings turn toward) is enough.
2. **"Hunts" or "keeps"? (POLISH, high).** After the midpoint the antagonist's intent is blurry. *Fix:* answer it with one image on Night 5. Lampwings carry a dancer off, and later Vesper finds that moth in a tiny case, alive and still. The hunt is the Mouth keeping them, which sets up its final offer.
3. **The Burn ending is hidden behind something players forget (POLISH).** Few players will remember the coal left at a lantern on Night 4. *Fix:* on Night 7 the coal sits in plain view at the lantern Dun guards, glowing faintly as she passes. Picking it up is the choice.
4. **The sister's timeline (POLISH).** *Fix:* add one object, a dated jar label or tally, showing she came back the next spring, already too late. The forty years then run from that night.
5. **Re-forming against a one-week life (POLISH).** *Fix:* this is already in the fiction, since lanterns are "old cocoons of her kind". Say that a lantern re-spins her and the night goes on. Make no further rule.
6. **Act III is crowded (POLISH).** On Night 7 the forest, Dun, Sorrel and Tallow each make a sacrifice in the same stretch, so every one of them lands with less weight. *Fix:* move the forest's heart going dark to the end of Night 6, seen from the kept city, so the last night belongs to Sorrel and the dish. Also fix the small points: the "she" in Night 1 should be Dun. On Night 5 Dun is "sent to carry her to a case", which makes the refusal readable. The final silk should pulse warm like the prologue's breath, so it reads as hatching and not as a threat.

No foundation problems.

## 5. Scores

Anchor 9 · Relationships 8 · Clarity 8 · Idea 9 · World 8 · Mechanics-as-theme 9 · Antagonist 9 · Ending 8 · Originality 8 · Grove fit 8 · Balance 8

On clarity: the reader got the spine, all six characters and all three endings right from memory. Their confusions are about details, not structure.

## 6. At the bar? Yes

I would put this outline next to the stories of Ori, Hollow Knight and Nine Sols as an equal. Its surface is a myth (a mouthless moth against a thing that swallowed the world). Under it is a precise technical truth (a starving dish of neurons that learned to stop the static). The reversal recolours choices the player has already made, and the cost is paid in the controller. The remaining risk is execution, not foundation: the light vocabulary has to be readable at a glance, because the moth's invisibility, the core of the owner's "underestimated" idea, depends on it.
