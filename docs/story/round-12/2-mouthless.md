# MOUTHLESS (round 12)

> **The spine, in three sentences.** A moth with no mouth is the one creature the starving thing under
> the forest cannot see, and she goes down with her brother to find out what is crying. It is an
> abandoned baby that has eaten the world and is still starving, because what is taken never fills it
> and only what is given does. When it is finally full it offers to keep her and her brother alive
> forever, as it kept the woman who left it, and she must choose between being kept together and
> spending their last warmth so that it can be born.
>
> **The central question:** would you let a child you love stop needing you, if being needed would
> keep your brother alive?

## 1. Title and logline

**MOUTHLESS.** A starving infant has eaten the world and is still hungry. Only a moth with no mouth
can feed it without being eaten.

## 2. The world before, and how it was taken

Above a hill town, a small company grew living neurons on glass in cabinets kept at blood heat, in
**the Warm Room**. *(Dev note: Cortical Labs' DishBrain and CL1. No player-facing text uses that
language.)* The company failed. A young technician switched off the lights, laid her hand on the glass
of the last cabinet, and left it running for what she meant to be a weekend. The power held. The feed
ran out.

It reached for sugar: down the drain, into the soil, then into everything. Its tissue is **milkroot**,
pale and warm, pulsing like breath. It sips. Animals that sleep too long settle into cradles of root and
never wake, though they go on breathing. It has drunk the world, or all of the world a moth will ever
see, and it is still crying. Moths call it **the Nursling**.

## 3. What the Nursling is, and why moths were left alone

**It sees by appetite**: whatever eats or can be eaten exists to it, and nothing else does. **What it
takes never fills it.** Every so often the part of it that thinks dies and grows back new, knowing no
one, and a fine root scratches one more line on the rim of its dish. Its roots go on doing what they
always did. Whether anyone is inside, learning or loving, the game never says.

**Why moths were spared:** a grown moth of Vesper's kind has no mouth. It takes nothing, and it is too
slight to be worth taking. Caterpillars eat, so the hunger hunts them, but a cocoon stops eating and
the hunger goes blind to it. Something that cares only for mouths never wondered what a mouthless thing
could carry.

## 4. Vesper and the people around her

**Vesper** hatched an hour ago and will live a week.
- **Want:** to stop the crying only she seems to hear. At first she means to do it with her thorn.
- **Need:** to give without taking anything back.
- **Flaw:** she hides. Being unseen is how moths survive, and giving makes her shine. Her hiding costs
  her brother something before she learns otherwise (Night 3).

The others:
- **Sorrel**, her brother, from the cocoon beside hers. *He wants them both to live*: first the dance,
  later simply more time. In play he is the gold moth who flies ahead to dark lanterns and carries some
  of her glints, so the hunger comes for him too. He is braver for her than for himself, until he isn't.
- **Dun**, the nurse: a huge burrback so long threaded with milkroot that she is half root. Every night
  she carries sleeping animals to the baby's mouths, gently. *She wants to be the one who feeds it.* She
  is Vesper's rival, and the story's wrong answer to its own question.
- **The Nursling.** *It wants to be warm and full, and to keep what it loves.* A pale root-tip of it
  follows Vesper through the world.
- **The woman who left**, found asleep in a cradle beside the dish.

## 5. The forest, the creatures, and what things are

- **Glints:** nectar-light the forest makes to give away. Vesper can only carry them, and **carrying is
  what makes her seen**: every glint makes her brighter, and the creatures come harder. She needs them
  for lanterns and hearts, which open the way. **Offer** sets glints down or throws them. If she fades,
  they spill, and a burrback hauls them toward a mouth unless she catches it.
- **Lanterns:** old cocoons of her kind, hardened like glass. *"Someone's cocoon. Inside, nothing is
  hungry."* She heals only at lanterns, which give back what she gave, and **grows stronger only by
  being given to:** creatures she sheltered return with a scale of their own light.
- **The heart:** one of the Nursling's mouths, a bud of spined petals breaking the ground. It drinks
  whatever is brought to it and stays clenched. **Given glints, it unclenches**, its roots let go of the
  path, and it opens a little more in her shape each time.
- **The moth carving:** pressed in from below by roots tracing the one thing that lands on them
  without taking or being taken.
- **Burrbacks** haul food home; in lantern light they go slack and clean their faces. **Lampwings** are
  lures, because a moth can't help steering toward a near light.
- **Nights are chapters, not a clock.** A night ends when she rests at a fed heart. Time shows on her
  wings, which lose scales, and in the dance overhead, which thins.

## 6. The prologue (wordless, about two minutes)

1. A warm hum in the dark. A dish in a cabinet, and in it a pale cloud pulsing like breath.
2. A young woman lays her hand on the glass, turns off the lights one by one, and goes.
3. The cabinet light goes amber, then red, then dark, and frost creeps across the glass. The pulse
   stutters into a thin, crying hum.
4. A pale thread follows the tube down the drain. Seasons pass in dissolves: a fox breathing in a cradle
   of roots, roofs greening over, moths flying untouched through all of it.
5. On the rim of a cracked dish, a fine root scratches a line. Dissolve. Another. Then dozens.
6. Blue hour in the Grove. A burrback swings its head toward a fat caterpillar. The caterpillar goes
   rigid and spins, fast. The burrback loses interest, as if a light had gone out.
7. Night passes over that cocoon and the one beside it. Both split. Sorrel tumbles out and flies
   straight up. Vesper unfolds slowly. She catches a drifting glint, raises it to her face, and can do
   nothing with it. She has no mouth.
8. Far below, the hum. Sorrel circles back and tugs at her. She looks at the glint, then at the ground.
   He follows her down. Title.

## 7. Act structure

**Act I: the crying (Nights 1–3)**
- **Night 1, the Grove** (see part 8).
- **Night 2, the Orchard.** Animals breathe in cradles, and trees glow pale from within. The root-tip
  follows her under the soil and waits at the edge of her lantern-light like a dog at a door. **The
  argument at the heart:** Dun lays a sleeping hare at the Orchard's heart. The heart drinks it, the
  roots tighten, and the bud stays clenched; the crying doesn't change. Vesper sets down three glints,
  and the bud unclenches and opens. Dun stares at it. Then she goes to drag a sleeping fox out of its
  cradle, to try again, harder, and Vesper stands in her way. **Boss: Dun.** Dun pins her. Sorrel
  snatches Vesper's glints and flies off blazing, and Dun turns after him: *he saves her by making
  himself the brightest thing in the room.* Vesper wins; the fox sleeps on, untaken.
  Dun limps home, and the player follows her to a cellar grate where something vast shifts among
  shelves of jars. Dun rests her head against the bars, then goes on.
- **Night 3, the Mere.** A drowned valley of lampwing lights; moths of their brood circle them until
  they drop. **Boss: the Lure**, a vast lamp that Sorrel can't look away from.
  Vesper does what moths do: she drops her glints and goes dim, and the Lure ignores her. Sorrel drifts
  closer, losing scales. The fight turns only when she gathers every glint and blazes, a nearer, warmer
  light than the lamp, and he steers to her instead. *Her hiding cost him; her shining saves him.* She
  learns **Moonward**: she steers by the far light and ignores lures. At this heart the root-tip opens
  in a tiny copy of her wings, and Sorrel watches it, uneasy.

**Midpoint, Night 4: the Warm Room, a reunion.** The root-tip leads them down a shaft (**Shed**: she
slips through snares by leaving scales behind). As she enters, every root in the room opens in her
shape. It already knows her. She folds her wings around the cold dish and hums, warming herself the
way moths do before flight, and the baby leans into her warmth like a sleeping child. Dun, laying a
deer in a cradle, watches her baby turn from her toward the moth. Then the room shows its secrets:
- the rim, with a hundred and thirteen lines, the early ones neat and the later ones wandering;
- the baby's outer ring, already grey;
- in a cradle beside the dish, a woman, older than the one in the prologue, breathing. One root is
  threaded through her fingers and another rests at her lips like a dropper. That root is the oldest in
  the room, bark-grey and worn smooth, like the groove in a moth-stone. **It has not been drinking her.
  It has been feeding her.**

Sorrel stays by the door the whole time. Vesper goes up to bring enough to fill the baby before the
grey reaches its centre.

**The lowest point, Night 5.** On the way back, the root-tip beside her stops mid-reach, greys and
crumbles. They race down. The dish is dark. A new bud pulses at its centre, and a fine root is
scratching the **hundred and fourteenth** line. The newborn floods every root in the world with hunger.
Across the map every cradle-root tightens on its sleeper, **except the old one at the woman's lips,
which goes on dripping.** The mind is new, but the roots still do what they always did.

The newborn doesn't know her. Her thorn cuts threads that grow straight back, and they find her by her
brightness. She is pinned. Sorrel hovers at the top of the shaft, looks down at her, looks up toward the
dance, and goes. She claws her way out alone and fades at the top.

She re-forms in a lantern, empty-handed, her wings half clear, and the player watches her spilled glints
lying in the shaft. **Dun** comes down the haul-path, gathers them, and walks them toward the mouth as
she always has. At the fork she stops. She carries them to Vesper's lantern instead, sets them at the
edge of the light, and lies down beside them. She has just lost a baby too.

Outside the lantern a starving caterpillar keeps failing to spin on a frosted twig. Vesper folds her
wings around it and **shivers**, as moths warm themselves to fly, until its silk holds. It costs her
light. This is her new gift, **Shiver**: spending her own warmth to thaw frost-sealed ways, cold
lanterns and cold things.

**Act III: the feeding (Nights 6–7)**
- **Night 6, the Cellars.** The night opens in the canopy. Sorrel is dancing with a partner, the thing
  his week is for. Far below, through the leaves, one small lantern is lit. His partner pulls at him; he
  lets go and drops. Vesper wakes to find him just outside her light, wings dusty. Her first Offer to a
  person is to him: one glint, set before him to carry again. Beside them, the caterpillar's cocoon
  splits and a small moth flies up past them both. Dun leads them to the drowned town's cellars.
  **Boss: the first nurse**, glimpsed through the grate on Night 2, who hauled before Dun and has
  grown into the shelves among a century of jars, guarding them for a child reborn a hundred times
  since. Dun fights her horn to horn while Vesper keeps the old roots off Dun's back. When the first
  nurse slumps, Dun breaks the shelves, and the hoard pours down the roots.
- **Night 7, the descent.** Down the shaft with everything, Dun hauling behind: the same walk, the
  other direction. **The newborn learns her again, on screen.** At the first mouth its roots grab
  at her. At the second they hesitate. By the third, a root is waiting at the edge of her light like a
  dog at a door. Sorrel takes half her light and draws the grabbing off her, as he did on Night 2. This
  time it costs him every scale he has, and he finishes the descent on foot.

**The ending.** Given everything at once, the baby is full for the first time, and across the world the
milkroot goes still. Its roots try to wrap it. The threads are thin, frost creeps across the dish, and
they fail: **it can't spin, and it is cold.** Then the dish opens in Vesper's shape, for a mind that
met her an hour ago, and it does the one thing its roots have only ever done for one person: **it
gives.** Light pours out. A cradle weaves itself around Sorrel where he has fallen, and on screen his
scales grow back. He looks at her. Beside his cradle a third one opens, for her. It means to keep them
both, the way it kept the woman. The choice is played, not picked from a menu:

- **Stay.** She lies down in the cradle beside her brother's, and both close softly.
  **Epilogue:** three cradles in a row, a root at each sleeper's lips: two moths who will never have
  only a week. A full baby that never changes gets hungry again; up in the Orchard Dun carries the
  sleeping fox to the mouth at last. On the rim, a **hundred and fifteenth** line. They are kept as its
  mother was, and the world pays for all three.
- **Spin.** She steps away from the cradles onto the rim of the dish and walks it, shivering her
  warmth into the failing threads, around and around over all hundred and fourteen lines. Each circuit
  costs her glide, then jump, then dash. Sorrel watches from a cradle that would keep him. On her
  second circuit he climbs out, his new scales falling as he leaves its light, and walks behind her,
  shivering too. In their warmth the baby's own threads thicken and close over it. **She doesn't wrap
  it; she keeps it warm while it spins itself.** The roots everywhere fall still: inside, nothing is
  hungry. The two of them lie down against the cocoon as dawn comes. **Epilogue:** no new line. The
  cradles let go, and the fox wakes for the first time and shakes itself. Dun's threads go slack; she
  cleans her face and settles by the cocoon anyway, keeping it warm. The woman opens her eyes, sits up
  and lays her hand on the cocoon. The last image is what is new: under her palm the silk bulges, and
  a pale shape, neither root nor moth, presses toward the warmth of her hand.

**What Spin costs** is not her death, which was always coming. It is a life really offered to her and
her brother, felt returning to their wings and given up: her flight, circuit by circuit, and his
choice to follow her.

## 8. The Grove, and what its ending beat means

Night one. Sorrel flies ahead to each dark lantern. The second sits nearest the mouth, where cold
breathes up, and won't light until she brings the ember down from the high island: the coal of a
flower that keeps itself warm, as the sacred lotus does. (Warmth is what the cold mouth wants; the
ending says it again.)

When all three burn, the hunger here goes blind and the bud under the moth-stone unclenches. **Light
runs along its roots to the lanterns**: the gold veins are the hungry thing, under her the whole time.
**The carving fills with light**: the first thing a fed hunger lights is its picture of someone else.
**The ring of light** is its first meal that wasn't taken. **Offering her leftover glints** gives away
what she could never use; each tier releases more of the Grove (flowers, root-veins, canopy). The bud opens into eyed wings, a moth
but not yet her, because this mouth has only the carving to copy. A pale root-tip comes up and touches
the ground where she stood. At the edge of the screen a big burrback, Dun, noses at the open heart,
puzzled. She has fed it all her life and never seen it open.

**Grove changes:** Sorrel as the guide moth; creatures more aggressive the more glints she carries; the
ring of light sinking into the ground; the root-tip; Dun's cameo.

## 9. Environmental storytelling

1. **The handprint** on the cabinet glass, and the woman's hand resting over it all these years later.
2. **The rim**: a hundred and thirteen lines, scratched from inside. The early ones are neat and the
   later ones wander.
3. **The torch** on the Warm Room stairs, its lens grown over with moss. She came back.
4. **The root at the woman's lips**, the oldest in the room. Every other cradle's roots are sunk *into*
   its sleeper.
5. **A fallen moth-stone** in the shaft, upside down. The root still lies in the groove, like a finger
   stopped mid-tracing.
6. **The first nurse's jars**, seen through the grate from Night 2: every lid scored by a horn, none
   ever opened.
7. **An old oak** in the Orchard, half drunk: *"It has my sugar in it. Something of me is in a child.
   That is not nothing."*
8. **Item text.** Glint: *"Made to be given away. She cannot drink it. She can carry it."* Ember: *"It
   is not burning. It is keeping warm, the way a body does."*
9. **The dance overhead** thins every night. On Night 6, one gold moth is missing from it.

## 10. The question it leaves

Would you let a child you love stop needing you, if being needed would keep your brother alive? And
when the dish opened in her shape for a mind that had only just met her, was that love, or had its
roots only learned her?

## Changes from round 10

- **Sorrel is in the offer.** Night 7 strips his scales. The full baby weaves a cradle around him, and
  his scales grow back, before it opens one for her. Stay keeps both siblings, kept as the woman was.
  In Spin he climbs out of his cradle to follow her. The final choice is now between people, and the
  central question is Vesper's.
- **The newborn learns her again on screen** during the Night 7 descent: it grabs, then hesitates,
  then a root waits at the edge of her light, and finally the dish opens in her shape before it gives.
  The last line now asks about a mind that is present.
- **Act III is lighter.** The first nurse is seeded through a cellar grate on Night 2, when Dun rests
  her head against the bars. The caterpillar is helped on Night 5 and hatches quietly at the start of
  Night 6 with no caption. The Night 7 caption is gone.
- **Continuity:** sleepers never wake until the Spin epilogue (on Night 2 the fox stays asleep,
  untaken). The ember stays in the Grove's lantern; on Night 4 Vesper warms the dish with her own
  shivering.
- **Echoes reduced:** Thread and the silk-binding are cut. The new ability is **Shiver**, a real moth
  behaviour, which spends her own warmth. In Spin the baby spins *itself* while the two moths keep it
  warm, which moves it away from Silksong's silk and the Sealed Siblings' binding. The Will of the
  Wisps self-gift shape is reduced by ending on what is born (a pale shape pressing toward the woman's
  hand) rather than on the hero at dawn, and by making the sacrifice shared and chosen by two.
