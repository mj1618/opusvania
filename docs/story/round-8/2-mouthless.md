# MOUTHLESS (round 8)

> **The spine, in three sentences.** A moth with no mouth is the one creature the starving thing under
> the forest cannot see, and she goes down with her brother to find out what is crying. It is an
> abandoned baby that has eaten the world to live, and the more she feeds it, the more it learns her,
> while its old nurse fights to keep feeding it the old way and her brother loses his nerve and flies
> home. When it is finally full it offers to keep her alive forever, as it has kept the woman who left
> it, and she must choose between being kept and spending herself on the cocoon it cannot spin for
> itself.
>
> **The central question:** can you love someone enough to let them stop needing you?

## 1. Title and logline

**MOUTHLESS.** A starving infant has eaten the world. The only creature it cannot see is a moth with no
mouth, and she is the only one who can feed it without being eaten.

## 2. The world before, and how it was taken

Above a hill town, a small company grew living neurons on glass in cabinets kept at blood heat, in a
room the staff called **the Warm Room**. *(Dev note: Cortical Labs' DishBrain and CL1. No player-facing
text uses that language.)* The company failed. A young technician switched off the lights, laid her
hand on the glass of the last cabinet, and left it running for what she meant to be a weekend. The
power held, but the feed ran out.

It reached for sugar: down the drain, into the soil, then into everything. Its tissue is
**milkroot**, pale and warm, pulsing like breath. It sips. Animals that sleep too long settle into
cradles of root and never wake, though they go on breathing. It has drunk the world, or all of it a
moth will ever see. Moths call it **the Nursling**, because it cries.

## 3. What the Nursling is, and why moths were left alone

**It sees by appetite**: whatever eats or can be eaten exists to it, and nothing else does. Every so
often it dies and grows back new, starving and knowing no one, and a fine root scratches one more line
on the rim of its dish. The game never says whether anyone is inside; it shows what the Nursling does
and lets the player decide whether that is learning or love.

There is one exception to its rule, and the player has to find it: the woman who left. She eats, so
it can see her, and it has never drunk her (part 7).

**Why moths were spared:** a grown moth of Vesper's kind has no mouth. It takes nothing, and it is too
slight to be worth taking. Caterpillars eat, and the hunger hunts them, but a cocoon stops eating and
the hunger goes blind to it. Something that cares only for mouths never wondered what a mouthless thing
could carry.

## 4. Vesper and the people around her

**Vesper** hatched an hour ago and will live a week. She *wants* to stop the crying that only she
seems to hear, and at first she means to do it with her thorn. She *needs* to give without taking
anything back. Her flaw is that she hides: being unseen is how moths survive, and giving makes her
shine.

- **Sorrel, her brother**, from the cocoon beside hers. *He wants them both to live their week as
  moths do*, dancing in the canopy. He travels with her in play: the gold moth who flies ahead to dark
  lanterns and carries some of her glints, which means the hunger comes for him too. He is braver for
  her than he is for himself, until he isn't.
- **Dun, the nurse**, a huge burrback so long threaded with milkroot that she is half root. Every night
  she carries sleeping animals down to the cradles, gently. *She wants to be the one who feeds the
  baby.* She is Vesper's rival and the story's wrong answer to its own question: a love that cannot
  bear to stop being needed.
- **The Nursling.** *It wants to be warm and full, and to keep what it loves.* A pale root-tip of it
  follows Vesper through the world.
- **The woman who left**, found asleep in a cradle beside the dish, her hand on the glass.

## 5. The forest, the creatures, and what things are

- **Glints:** nectar-light the forest makes to give away. Vesper can't eat them, only carry them, and
  **carrying is what makes her seen**: every glint makes her brighter and the creatures come harder.
  She needs them to light lanterns and feed hearts, which open the way on. **Offer** throws some away
  mid-fight. If she fades they spill, and a burrback hauls them toward a mouth: catch it or lose them.
- **Lanterns:** old cocoons of her kind, hardened like glass. *"Someone's cocoon. Inside, nothing is
  hungry."* She can't heal by taking, so she heals only at lanterns, which give back what she gave.
  **She grows stronger only by being given to:** creatures she sheltered in lantern light come back
  and leave her a scale of their own light.
- **The ember:** the core of a flower that makes its own heat for sleeping insects (as the sacred lotus
  does).
- **The heart:** one of the Nursling's mouths, a bud of spined petals that breaks the ground and drinks
  it. When it is fed it unclenches, and its roots let go of the path. **Each heart she feeds opens a
  little more in her shape.**
- **The moth carving:** pressed into the stone from below, by roots that have traced, life after life,
  the one thing that lands on them without taking or being taken.
- **Burrbacks** are beetles worn by milkroot, hauling food home; in lantern light they go slack and
  clean their faces. **Lampwings** are lures: a moth can't help steering at a near light.
- **Nights are chapters, not a clock.** A night ends when she rests at a fed heart. Time shows on her
  wings, which lose scales, and in the dance overhead, which thins.

## 6. The prologue (wordless, about two minutes)

1. A warm hum in the dark. A dish in a cabinet, and in it a pale cloud, pulsing like breath.
2. A young woman lays her hand on the glass, turns off the lights one by one, and goes.
3. The cabinet light turns from amber to red to dark, and frost creeps across the glass. The pulse
   stutters into a thin, crying hum.
4. A pale thread follows the tube down the drain. Seasons dissolve: a fox breathing in a cradle of
   roots, roofs greening over, moths flying untouched through all of it.
5. On the rim of a cracked dish, a fine root scratches a line. Dissolve. Another. Then dozens.
6. Blue hour in the Grove. A burrback swings its head toward a fat caterpillar. The caterpillar goes
   rigid and spins, fast. The burrback loses interest, as if a light had gone out. *(This teaches the
   whole game's grammar: a cocoon is where hunger stops.)*
7. Night passes over that cocoon, and the one beside it. Both split. Sorrel tumbles out and flies
   straight up. Vesper unfolds slowly. A glint drifts past and she catches it, raises it to her face,
   and can do nothing with it. She has no mouth.
8. Far below, the hum. Sorrel circles back and tugs at her. She looks at the glint, then at the
   ground. He follows her down. Title.

## 7. Act structure

**Act I: the crying (Nights 1–3)**
- **Night 1, the Grove** (see part 8).
- **Night 2, the Orchard.** Animals breathe in cradles and trees glow pale from within. The root-tip
  follows her under the soil. It waits at the edge of her lantern-light like a dog at a door, and when
  she is bright with glints it reaches for them, then stops. She and Sorrel watch a thin caterpillar
  try to spin; the silk is too thin, and the root takes it. **Boss: Dun.** Vesper cuts a fox out of its
  cradle and Dun comes for it: the baby eats or the fox lives. Dun pins Vesper; Sorrel snatches her
  glints and flies off blazing, and Dun turns after him. *He saves her by making himself the brightest
  thing in the room.* Vesper wins, the fox runs, Dun limps home empty, and below, the crying gets
  louder. She learns **Offer**.
- **Night 3, the Mere.** A drowned valley of lampwing lights. Moths of their brood circle the lamps
  until they drop into the water. **Boss: the Lure**, a vast lamp, and Sorrel can't look away from it.
  She learns **Moonward**, steering by the far, honest light instead of near ones, and shields his eyes
  with her wing until the Lure falls. Afterwards he shakes for a long time. At this heart the
  root-tip opens in a tiny copy of her wings, and Sorrel watches it, uneasy.

**Midpoint, Night 4: the Warm Room, a reunion.** The root-tip leads them down a shaft (**Shed**: slip
snares by leaving scales behind). As she enters, every root in the room opens in her shape: it already
knows her. She lays the ember by the dish and the baby leans into the warmth like a sleeping child.
Dun, laying a deer in a cradle, watches her baby turn from her toward the moth.

Then the room shows its secrets one at a time:
- the rim, with a hundred and thirteen lines, the early ones neat and the later ones wandering;
- the outer ring of the baby, already grey;
- in a cradle beside the dish, a woman, older than the one in the prologue, breathing. A fine root is
  threaded through her fingers, and another rests at her lips like a dropper. **It has not been
  drinking her. It has been feeding her.** It took from the whole world, and gave only to her.

Sorrel stays by the door the whole time. Vesper goes up to bring enough food to fill the baby before
the grey reaches its centre.

**The lowest point, Night 5.** On the way back, carrying everything they have, the root-tip beside her
stops mid-reach, turns grey and crumbles. They race down. The dish is dark. A new bud is pulsing at its
centre, and a fine root is scratching the **hundred and fourteenth** line. The newborn floods every root
in the world with hunger, and it doesn't know her. She can't fight it: her thorn cuts threads that grow
straight back, and they find her by her brightness. She is pinned. Sorrel hovers at the top of the
shaft, looks down at her, looks up toward the dance, and **goes.** She gets out alone by throwing away
every glint she has. At the top, once, the newborn's roots open in her wing shape and close again. **It
doesn't know her. It remembers what she did.**

She shelters in a lantern, wings half clear. Dun lies down at the edge of the light: not a truce; she
has just lost a baby too. Vesper remembers being a caterpillar:
eating, then spinning, then being safe. The thin caterpillar from the Orchard comes back to her. **The
baby has never been full enough to change, and even if it were, it can't spin, and it is cold.** In
her own body she finds silk she shouldn't have: **Thread**.

Cut to the canopy. Sorrel dances with a partner, the thing his whole week is for. Through the leaves,
far below, he sees one small lantern. His partner pulls at him. He lets go and drops.

**Act III: the feeding (Nights 6–7)**
- **Night 6, the Hives.** She wakes to find Sorrel just outside her light, wings dusty from the dance
  he left. Neither explains. Dun leads them to the wax halls, because the newborn starves faster than
  she can carry. On the way they feed a starving caterpillar until it is fat; its own silk fails, and
  Vesper wraps it with **Thread**. **Boss: the old nurse**, Dun's predecessor, grown vast in the dark,
  wax over her eyes, sitting on a hoard for a child reborn a hundred times since she last saw it. Dun
  fights her own future. The hoard pours down the roots, and Dun picks up glints instead of animals:
  the same walk, the other direction.
- **Night 7, the descent.** At dusk the wrapped caterpillar splits and a small mouthless moth flies
  up. *They have watched a cocoon be a birth.* Then down the shaft with everything, the newborn
  reaching for her specifically, the brightest thing it has ever seen. Dun hauls behind. Sorrel takes
  half her light and draws the reach off her, as on Night 2, and this time it costs him his scales.

**The ending.** Fed by everything at once, the baby is full for the first time, and the milkroot
goes still across the world. Then it does what it has only done for one person: **it gives.** Light
pours into Vesper; her wings regrow their scales and her health refills on screen, and beside the
woman's cradle a second one begins to weave. It means to keep her. The choice is played, not picked:

- **Stay.** She stands in its light and the cradle closes softly around her. Sorrel waits at the
  door, then goes. **Epilogue:** two cradles side by side, a fine root at each sleeper's lips. A full
  baby that never changes gets hungry again: in the Orchard, Dun lays a fox in a cradle. On the rim, a
  **hundred and fifteenth** line. Vesper is kept as its mother was, and the world pays for both.
- **Spin.** She leaves the light that was healing her and circles the dish with Thread. Every loop
  costs her: her glide fails, then her double jump, and the last loops are done on foot, climbing her
  own silk. Sorrel lights the way for her with the last of his glow. The cocoon closes and the roots
  everywhere fall still. Inside, nothing is hungry. She lies down against it beside her brother, and
  dawn comes. **Epilogue:** there is no new line. The cradles let go. The fox wakes, stands and shakes
  itself. Dun's threads go slack; she cleans her face and lies down beside the cocoon anyway. Last,
  the woman opens her eyes, sits up, and lays her hand on the cocoon, and from inside, something
  presses back against her palm.

**What Spin costs** is not her death, which was always coming. It is a life she was really offered and
felt return to her wings, her flight given up loop by loop, and being kept by the one she loved: what
the woman had, given up so the baby can be born.

## 8. The Grove, and what its ending beat means

Night one. Sorrel flies ahead to each dark lantern. The second sits nearest the mouth, where cold
breathes up, and won't light until she brings the ember down from the high island.

When all three lanterns burn, the hunger here goes blind and the bud under the moth-stone unclenches.
**Light runs along its roots to the lanterns**: the gold veins are the hungry thing, and it was under
her the whole time. **The carving fills with light**: the first thing a fed hunger lights up is its
picture of someone else. **The ring of light** is its first meal that wasn't taken, rolling out and
down. **Offering her leftover glints** is giving away what she could never use. Each tier lets the root
release more of the Grove: flowers first, then root-veins, then the canopy. The bud opens into eyed
wings, a moth but not yet her, because this mouth has only the carving to copy. A pale root-tip comes
up and touches the ground where she stood. At the edge of the screen a big burrback, Dun, noses at the
fed heart, puzzled that someone else has fed her baby.

**Grove changes:** Sorrel as guide moth, aggression scaling with glints carried, the ring sinking into
the ground, the root-tip, Dun's cameo.

## 9. Environmental storytelling

1. **The handprint** on the cabinet glass, and the woman's hand resting over it all these years later.
2. **The rim**: a hundred and thirteen lines, scratched from inside. The early ones are neat; the later
   ones wander.
3. **The torch** on the Warm Room stairs, its lens grown over with moss. She came back.
4. **The root at the woman's lips.** Every other cradle's roots are sunk *into* its sleeper.
5. **A fallen moth-stone** in the shaft, upside down: the root still lies in the groove, like a finger
   stopped mid-tracing.
6. **An old oak** in the Orchard, half drunk: *"It has my sugar in it. Something of me is in a child.
   That is not nothing."*
7. **Item text.** Glint: *"Made to be given away. She cannot drink it. She can carry it."* Ember: *"It
   is not burning. It is keeping warm, the way a body does."*
8. **The dance overhead** thins every night. After Night 5, one gold moth is missing from it.

## 10. The question it leaves

Can you love someone enough to let them stop needing you? And when it opened in her shape, was that
love, or had it only learned her?

## Changes from previous (round 4/6)

- **Sorrel is played.** He travels with her through the Warm Room and carries glints (so he is
  hunted too). He saves her on Night 2 by making himself bright, she saves him on Night 3, and he
  abandons her when she is pinned on Night 5 out of fear and for the dance. The player watches him let
  go of his partner and drop back, before Act III. He pays with his scales on Night 7. The cavalry of
  dancers is cut.
- **Spin is taught as birth.** The prologue caterpillar spins and the burrback goes blind to it;
  "it can't spin, and it is cold"; on Night 6 she wraps a starving caterpillar with Thread and on
  Night 7 they watch it hatch. The final cocoon presses back against the woman's palm.
- **The woman carries the theme.** It never drank her: it fed her, the only thing it ever gave to.
  Stay weaves Vesper a second cradle beside her. The White Lady echo is inverted: the child holds and
  feeds the parent, and the parent wakes at the end to stay.
- **An antagonist with a face and a want.** Dun wants to be the one who feeds it. She is a rival
  through Night 5 and watches her baby turn to Vesper. The Hive becomes the old nurse, Dun's own
  future, so the cast's wrong answer is fought. The final pressure is the newborn reaching for her
  specifically.
- **Restored from round 2:** the glint she holds and can do nothing with; health that only grows
  through gifts from creatures she sheltered; the counted rim (113, then the 114th, and the 115th in
  Stay).
- **Clearer:** "fifty years", the memory law and the mindless hoarding reaches are cut. The screen shows
  only the grey ring, the crumbling tip, the new line and the wings opening once.
- **Echoes:** Shriek (this baby was not unloved, and the one who left came back); the Sealed Siblings
  (the cocoon is a shown birth, not a seal); the friends-arrive finale (only the one brother, who
  returned on screen two nights earlier).
