# MOUTHLESS (round 10)

> **The spine, in three sentences.** A moth with no mouth is the one creature the starving thing under
> the forest cannot see, and she goes down with her brother to find out what is crying. It is an
> abandoned baby that has eaten the world and is still starving, and its old nurse keeps feeding it the
> only way she knows, while Vesper learns that it can only be filled by what is given. When it is
> finally full it offers to keep her alive forever, as it has kept the woman who left it, and she must
> choose between being kept and spending herself on the cocoon it cannot spin for itself.
>
> **The central question:** can you love someone enough to let them stop needing you?

## 1. Title and logline

**MOUTHLESS.** A starving infant has eaten the world and is still hungry. The only creature it cannot
see is a moth with no mouth, and she is the only one who can feed it without being eaten.

## 2. The world before, and how it was taken

Above a hill town, a small company grew living neurons on glass in cabinets kept at blood heat, in
**the Warm Room**. *(Dev note: Cortical Labs' DishBrain and CL1. No player-facing
text uses that language.)* The company failed. A young technician switched off the lights, laid her
hand on the glass of the last cabinet, and left it running for what she meant to be a weekend. The
power held. The feed ran out.

It reached for sugar: down the drain, into the soil, then into everything. Its tissue is **milkroot**,
pale and warm and pulsing like breath. It sips. Animals that sleep too long settle into cradles of root
and never wake, though they go on breathing. It has drunk the world, or all of the world a moth will
ever see, and it is still crying. Moths call it **the Nursling**.

## 3. What the Nursling is, and why moths were left alone

**It sees by appetite**: whatever eats or can be eaten exists to it, and nothing else does. **What it
takes never fills it.** Every so often the part of it that thinks dies and grows back new, knowing no
one, and a fine root scratches one more line on the rim of its dish. Its roots go on doing what they
always did. The game never says whether anyone is inside; it shows what the Nursling does, and leaves
the player to decide whether that is learning or love.

**Why moths were spared:** a grown moth of Vesper's kind has no mouth. It takes nothing, and it is too
slight to be worth taking. Caterpillars eat, so the hunger hunts them, but a cocoon stops eating and the
hunger goes blind to it. Something that cares only for mouths never wondered what a mouthless thing
could carry.

## 4. Vesper and the people around her

**Vesper** hatched an hour ago and will live a week.
- **Want:** to stop the crying only she seems to hear. At first she means to do it with her thorn.
- **Need:** to give without taking anything back.
- **Flaw:** she hides. Being unseen is how moths survive, and giving makes her shine. Her hiding costs
  her brother something before she learns otherwise (Night 3).

The others:
- **Sorrel**, her brother, from the cocoon beside hers. *He wants them both to live their week as moths
  do*, dancing in the canopy. In play he is the gold moth who flies ahead to dark lanterns and carries
  some of her glints, so the hunger comes for him too. He is braver for her than for himself, until he
  isn't.
- **Dun**, the nurse: a huge burrback so long threaded with milkroot that she is half root. Every night
  she carries sleeping animals to the baby's mouths, gently. *She wants to be the one who feeds it.* She
  is Vesper's rival and the story's wrong answer to its own question: a love that can't bear to stop
  being needed.
- **The Nursling.** *It wants to be warm and full, and to keep what it loves.* A pale root-tip of it
  follows Vesper through the world.
- **The woman who left**, found asleep in a cradle beside the dish.

## 5. The forest, the creatures, and what things are

- **Glints:** nectar-light the forest makes to give away. Vesper can only carry them, and **carrying
  is what makes her seen**: each glint makes her brighter and the creatures come harder. She needs them
  for lanterns and hearts, which open the way. **Offer** sets them down or throws them. If she fades
  they spill, and a burrback hauls them toward a mouth unless she catches it.
- **Lanterns:** old cocoons of her kind, hardened like glass. *"Someone's cocoon. Inside, nothing is
  hungry."* She can't heal by taking, so she heals only at lanterns, which give back what she gave
  them. **She grows stronger only by being given to:** creatures she sheltered come back and leave her
  a scale of their own light.
- **The heart:** one of the Nursling's mouths, a bud of spined petals that breaks through the ground.
  It drinks what is brought to it and stays clenched. **Given glints, it unclenches**, its roots let
  go of the path, and it opens a little more in her shape each time.
- **The moth carving:** pressed into the stone from below by roots that, life after life, have traced
  the one thing that lands on them without taking or being taken.
- **Burrbacks** haul food home; in lantern light they go slack and clean their faces. **Lampwings** are
  lures: a moth can't help steering toward a near light.
- **Nights are chapters, not a clock.** A night ends when she rests at a fed heart. Time shows on her
  wings, which lose scales, and in the dance overhead, which thins.

## 6. The prologue (wordless, about two minutes)

1. A warm hum in the dark. A dish in a cabinet, and in it a pale cloud pulsing like breath.
2. A young woman lays her hand on the glass, turns off the lights one by one, and goes.
3. The cabinet light turns amber, then red, then dark, and frost creeps across the glass. The pulse
   stutters into a thin, crying hum.
4. A pale thread follows the tube down the drain. Seasons pass in dissolves: a fox breathing in a cradle
   of roots, roofs greening over, moths flying untouched through all of it.
5. On the rim of a cracked dish, a fine root scratches a line. Dissolve. Another. Then dozens.
6. Blue hour in the Grove. A burrback swings its head toward a fat caterpillar. The caterpillar goes
   rigid and spins, fast. The burrback loses interest, as if a light had gone out.
7. Night passes over that cocoon and the one beside it. Both split. Sorrel tumbles out and flies
   straight up. Vesper unfolds slowly. She catches a drifting glint, raises it to her face, and can do
   nothing with it. She has no mouth.
8. Far below, the hum. Sorrel circles back and tugs at her. She looks at the glint, then at the ground.
   He follows her down. Title.

## 7. Act structure

**Act I: the crying (Nights 1–3)**
- **Night 1, the Grove** (see part 8).
- **Night 2, the Orchard.** Animals breathe in cradles and trees glow pale from within. The root-tip
  follows her under the soil and waits at the edge of her lantern-light like a dog at a door. **The
  argument at the heart:** Dun lays a sleeping hare at the Orchard's heart. The heart drinks it, the
  roots tighten, and the bud stays clenched; the crying doesn't change. Then Vesper sets down three
  glints. The bud unclenches and opens. Dun stares at it. Then she turns and goes for the fox in its
  cradle, to try again, harder. Vesper stands in her way. **Boss: Dun.** Dun pins her, and Sorrel
  snatches Vesper's glints and flies off blazing, so that Dun turns after him: *he saves her by
  making himself the brightest thing in the room.* Vesper wins and the fox runs. Dun limps home with
  nothing.
- **Night 3, the Mere.** A drowned valley of lampwing lights; moths of their brood circle them until
  they drop. **Boss: the Lure**, a vast lamp Sorrel can't look away from. Vesper does what moths do:
  drops her glints and goes dim, and the Lure ignores her. Sorrel drifts closer, losing scales. The
  fight turns only when she gathers every glint and blazes, a nearer, warmer light, and he steers to
  her instead. *Her hiding cost him. Shining saves him.* She learns **Moonward**, which lets her steer
  by the far light and ignore lures. At this heart the root-tip opens in a tiny copy of her wings, and
  Sorrel watches it, uneasy.

**Midpoint, Night 4: the Warm Room, a reunion.** The root-tip leads them down a shaft (**Shed**: she slips
through snares by leaving scales behind). When she enters, every root in the room opens in her shape: it
already knows her. She lays the ember by the dish, and the baby leans into the warmth like a sleeping
child. Dun, laying a deer in a cradle, watches her baby turn away from her toward the moth. Then the
room shows its secrets:
- the rim, with a hundred and thirteen lines, the early ones neat and the later ones wandering;
- the baby's outer ring, already grey;
- in a cradle beside the dish, a woman, older than the one in the prologue, breathing. A root is
  threaded through her fingers and another rests at her lips like a dropper. That root is the oldest
  in the room, bark-grey and worn smooth, like the groove in a moth-stone. **It has not been drinking
  her. It has been feeding her.**

Sorrel stays by the door the whole time. Vesper goes up to bring enough to fill the baby before the grey
reaches its centre.

**The lowest point, Night 5.** On the way back, the root-tip beside her stops mid-reach, greys and
crumbles. They race down. The dish is dark. A new bud pulses at its centre, and a fine root is
scratching the **hundred and fourteenth** line. The newborn floods every root in the world with hunger.
Across the map every cradle-root tightens on its sleeper, **except the old one at the woman's lips,
which goes on dripping.** The mind is new, but the roots still do what they always did.

The newborn doesn't know Vesper. Her thorn cuts threads that grow straight back, and they find her by
her brightness. She is pinned. Sorrel hovers at the top of the shaft. He looks down at her, looks up
toward the dance, and goes. She claws out alone and fades at the top.

She re-forms in a lantern, empty-handed, wings half clear, and the player watches her spilled glints
in the shaft. **Dun** comes down the haul-path, gathers them, and walks them toward the mouth as she
always has. At the fork she stops, then carries them to Vesper's lantern, sets them at the edge of the
light, and lies down beside them. She has just lost a baby too.

Vesper's wings rest. Inside her own body she finds silk she shouldn't have: **Thread**.

**Act III: the feeding (Nights 6–7)**
- **Night 6, the Cellars.** The night opens in the canopy: Sorrel dancing with a partner, the thing
  his week is for. Far below, through the leaves, one small lantern. His partner pulls; he lets go and
  drops. Vesper wakes to find him outside her light, wings dusty. Her first Offer to a person is to
  him: one glint, set before him to carry again. Dun leads them to the drowned town's cellars, because
  the newborn starves faster than she can haul. On the way they feed a starving caterpillar until it is
  fat, and when its own silk fails, Vesper wraps it in **Thread**. **Boss: the first nurse**, who hauled
  before Dun and has grown into the shelves among a century of jars, still guarding them for a child
  reborn a hundred times since. Dun fights her horn to horn while Vesper keeps the old roots off Dun's
  back. When the first nurse slumps, Dun breaks the shelves, and the hoard pours down the roots.
- **Night 7, the descent.** At dusk the wrapped caterpillar splits and a small mouthless moth flies up.
  *They have watched a cocoon be a birth.* Then they go down the shaft with everything. The newborn
  reaches for Vesper in particular, the brightest thing it has ever seen. Dun hauls behind her: the
  same walk, the other direction. Sorrel takes half her light and draws the reach off her, as he did on
  Night 2, and this time it costs him his scales.

**The ending.** Given everything at once, the baby is full for the first time, and the milkroot goes
still across the world. Its roots try to wrap it. The threads are thin, they fail, and frost creeps
across the dish: **it can't spin, and it is cold.** Then it does the one thing its roots have only ever
done for one person: **it gives.** Light pours into Vesper. On screen her wings regrow their scales and
her health refills, and beside the woman's cradle a second cradle begins to weave. It means to keep
her. The choice is played, not picked from a menu:

- **Stay.** She stands in its light, and the cradle closes softly around her. Sorrel waits at the door,
  then goes. **Epilogue:** two cradles side by side, a root at each sleeper's lips. A full baby that
  never changes gets hungry again, and up in the Orchard Dun lays a fox in a cradle. On the rim, a
  **hundred and fifteenth** line. Vesper is kept the way its mother was kept, and the world pays for
  both of them.
- **Spin.** She steps out of the healing light onto the rim of the dish and walks it, laying silk,
  around and around over all hundred and fourteen lines. Each circuit costs her glide, then jump, then
  dash. The last is a slow walk over the oldest, neatest lines, Sorrel beside her lighting the way with
  what glow he has left. The cocoon
  closes and the roots everywhere fall still: inside, nothing is hungry. She lies down against it
  beside her brother, and dawn comes. **Epilogue:** there is no new line. The cradles let go. The fox
  wakes, stands and shakes itself. Dun's threads go slack; she cleans her face, and lies down beside
  the cocoon anyway. Last, the woman opens her eyes, sits up and lays her hand on the cocoon, and from
  inside, something presses back against her palm.

**What Spin costs** is not her death, which was always coming. It costs a life she was really offered
and felt return to her wings, her flight given up circuit by circuit, and being kept by the one she
loved: what the woman had, given up so the baby can be born.

## 8. The Grove, and what its ending beat means

Night one. Sorrel flies ahead to each dark lantern. The second lantern sits nearest the mouth, where the
cold breathes up, and won't light until she brings the ember down from the high island: the coal of
a flower that keeps itself warm, as the sacred lotus does.

When all three burn, the hunger here goes blind and the bud under the moth-stone unclenches. **Light
runs along its roots to the lanterns**: the gold veins are the hungry thing, under her the whole time.
**The carving fills with light**: the first thing a fed hunger lights is its picture of someone else.
**The ring of light** is its first meal that wasn't taken, rolling out and down. **Offering her leftover
glints** is giving what she could never use; each tier lets the root release more of the Grove
(flowers, root-veins, canopy). The bud opens
into eyed wings, a moth but not yet her, because this mouth has only the carving to copy. A pale
root-tip comes up and touches the ground where she stood. At the edge of the screen a big burrback,
Dun, noses at the open heart, puzzled: she has fed it all her life and never seen it open.

**Grove changes:** Sorrel as the guide moth; creatures more aggressive the more glints she carries; the
ring of light sinking into the ground; the root-tip; Dun's cameo.

## 9. Environmental storytelling

1. **The handprint** on the cabinet glass, and the woman's hand resting over it all these years later.
2. **The rim**: a hundred and thirteen lines, scratched from inside. The early ones are neat, and the
   later ones wander.
3. **The torch** on the Warm Room stairs, its lens grown over with moss. She came back.
4. **The root at the woman's lips**, the oldest in the room. Every other cradle's roots are sunk *into*
   its sleeper.
5. **A fallen moth-stone** in the shaft, lying upside down. The root still lies in the groove, like a
   finger stopped mid-tracing.
6. **The first nurse's jars**: the town's preserves, every lid scored by a horn, none ever opened.
7. **An old oak** in the Orchard, half drunk: *"It has my sugar in it. Something of me is in a child.
   That is not nothing."*
8. **Item text.** Glint: *"Made to be given away. She cannot drink it. She can carry it."* Ember: *"It
   is not burning. It is keeping warm, the way a body does."*
9. **The dance overhead** thins every night. On Night 6, one gold moth is missing from it.

## 10. The question it leaves

Can you love someone enough to let them stop needing you? And when it opened in her shape, was that
love, or had it only learned her?

## Changes from round 8

- **The rule is shown on Night 2:** Dun's hare is drunk and the heart stays clenched, while Vesper's
  three glints open it. Taking never fills it and giving does, so the ending is set up well before it
  pays off, and the rivalry becomes a visible argument.
- **The logic hole is closed with one image:** on Night 5 every cradle-root tightens except the old one
  at the woman's lips, which keeps dripping. The roots keep the habit even though the mind is new. The
  caption "it remembers what she did" is cut.
- **Changes of heart now happen in play.** Dun's turn: when Vesper fades, Dun carries the spilled
  glints to her lantern instead of to the mouth. Sorrel's return is held until Night 6 opens, so she
  spends Night 5 alone, and her first Offer to a person is a glint for him to carry.
- **The flaw costs something:** at the Lure, hiding (going dim) costs Sorrel his scales, and only
  shining saves him. The spine's second sentence is now a single push and pull.
- **Less over-teaching:** two caterpillar demonstrations remain (the prologue spin, and the Night 6
  wrap with its Night 7 hatch). The Orchard failure and the flashback are cut. "It can't spin, and it
  is cold" becomes an image at the climax.
- **Echoes addressed:** the Hives and the waxed, blind matriarch become the drowned town's cellars and
  the first nurse rooted among unopened jars, and the fight is one Dun takes part in. Spin is no longer
  a summit climb: she walks the rim, laying silk over every tally, losing glide, then jump, then dash.
