# Review: 4. What the Lanterns Burn (round 15)

## 1. Anchor gate: PASS

A player's first sentence would be the litmus line almost word for word: a little moth against a living brain grown from human cells that put the world to sleep. The Hold is on screen and active at world scale in every act. In the prologue an eye in the rim takes a deer. In Act I its flesh pours over a grove while you run. In Act II every eye turns toward the ember, it grows the pale moth and it raids the moths' great tree. In Act III it builds the lure and grows the Hand. It learns new verbs as the game goes, so it has momentum. It acts through servants but never only through them.

- AI that took over the world: **central**. The origin is concrete: care of the dying, "a loss is the worst thing", pods, held forests.
- Moth trying to save the world: **central**. Both endings resolve the takeover.
- AI dismissed moths, underestimated Vesper: **central**. It is a stealth rule in play, it explains the Taper's old theft, and the escalation is the Hold correcting the mistake. "It never copies a moth that turns away from the light" pays it off.
- Sentience ambiguity: **central**. The sap-weeping eye and the skeleton rocked in the chair are paired, and the ending reads "Whether the Hold was someone decides which ending was the murder."

## 2. Craft verdict: GREENLIGHT WITH NOTES

The idea is strong and original, the central question is clear, and the relationships work through play. The problems are in rule density and Act II load, not in the foundation.

## 3. At the bar

- **An antagonist that is wrong in a way that lights up the hero.** "It did not understand goodbye. It held her." It is the Taper's flaw at the scale of the world, and the central question ("Can you love something without keeping it?") cuts through the Hold, the Taper and, without anyone saying so, the moths' own re-forming lanterns.
- **The ember as the one glint that finishes.** "Unlike every other glint, it finishes: the hand lifts away." A goodbye is the lesson the Hold never learned, carried by the creature it never looked at. This is the core of the mythology, and it is elegant.
- **The Pong dish grows up.** The pale moth "copies whatever Vesper last did... The Hold is learning her the way it learned the dot." The real-world Cortical Labs reference becomes a boss mechanic, and the counter (planting green under its landings) restates the theme: "it can copy her flight, not the forest."
- **The re-form is the relationship.** The Taper waits at the glass, then Vesper steps out alone, then it reverses. That is told entirely through a checkpoint, which is Ori-level economy.
- **The midpoint reframes the scene that already exists.** The glint "still glitters, so the player grabs it", then the sleeping face shows that "every lantern Vesper lit was burning one." The Grove table makes the second playthrough a different game.

## 4. Problems, ranked

1. **The lantern rules are overloaded (POLISH, high priority).** In one system, lanterns burn sleepers' thoughts, shrink the flesh, re-form moths (only those the Taper's wing has touched), reset a week-long life ("he steps out young"), and are snuffed as a cost. The outline never says what burning a glint does to the sleeper: does it lose a memory, a year, or its life? *Fix:* state one plain cost on screen (the sleeper's face in the pod goes still and its hand stops moving). Say that re-forming renews the week, and name that as the moths' own "keeping", so the moths are seen to share the Hold's flaw.
2. **Act II is overstuffed (POLISH).** It holds the ember, the turn, the pale moth, snuffing, Sedge's re-form, the raid and boss, the lowest point and moonward. *Fix:* cut the Sedge re-form beat ("he doesn't look at Vesper" then contradicts him following her), or fold it into the lowest point, where he is the one moth who doesn't turn away.
3. **Snuffing as checkpoint loss fights the genre (POLISH).** Losing save points as a moral tax will read as punishment in a metroidvania. *Fix:* planted rings act as re-form points that catch Vesper more slowly and dimly, so the kinder choice changes how play feels without making it worse.
4. **The ending is lopsided (POLISH).** *Burned* kills every human. Few players will feel it as a real option, which weakens "the game waits." *Fix:* make *Given back* visibly risky before the choice: in the room, the Hold briefly copies the hand lifting away and then re-closes it. That makes trusting it a real bet, not the obvious good ending.
5. **Clasp's turn is undermotivated (POLISH).** She folds over a cut pod, then turns her burrbacks away from the children. *Fix:* one played beat where Vesper plants green over one of Clasp's pods and the sleeper inside stirs, so her allegiance changes because of something the player did.
6. **The sleepers-in-pods image is familiar (POLISH).** Rows of people dreaming in pods will make some players think of *The Matrix*. *Fix:* lean on the specific images: one held moment per sleeper, the hand gestures, cradled deer, leaves hanging in the air. Avoid wide shots of pod walls.

No foundation problems.
