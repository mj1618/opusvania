# Raw ideation, L1, seed pack 3

Seed pack: deathwatch (vigil beside the dying; the beetle ticking in old timber), presider (one who presides over a meeting), *Sex, Death & the Infinite Void* (Creeper, 2020), words: azophenine, computative, gutturality, ungarnish, violableness, malleableize. Lens: **information is the main reward, not power.** Mood: **bureaucratic.**

Worked from the prompt only. PLAN.md and other concept files were not read.

---

## 1. Ten associations from the seeds

1. **Deathwatch as a job, not a mood.** Somebody is paid to sit beside the dying and write down what they say. The vigil is a hub you return to; going out is going out on the dying person's errands.
2. **The beetle in the timber.** Deathwatch beetles tap their heads on wood to find each other; folklore hears it as a clock. A whole building that ticks, and the ticking tells you where the wood is old enough to break through. Sound as a map of structural weakness.
3. **Presider = the person whose ruling makes a thing so.** A presider does not fight; a presider *rules*, and the room obeys the ruling. What if the player's verb is "rule on it" rather than "hit it"?
4. **"Infinite void" as the edge of the paperwork.** The world exists only as far as it has been filed. Past the last ledger entry there is literally nothing: not darkness, just unrecorded space. Recording is world-building in the most literal sense.
5. **"Sex, Death & the Infinite Void" as an inheritance drama.** Lovers, heirs, a death that hasn't happened yet, and a void where the estate ends. Grown-up soap opera stakes instead of "save the kingdom".
6. **Azophenine: a violet aniline dye.** The colour of official ink. Everything that has been recorded is stained violet; everything unrecorded is grey. A one-rule palette that can be done entirely in shader and silhouette.
7. **Computative: the world is a running tally.** Lifts driven by adding machines, doors opened by totals, enemies that are sums that haven't been carried yet. Assessment as combat: you compute what something is owed.
8. **Gutturality: the death rattle; the voice of things with no name.** Unnamed things speak from the throat; naming them (filing them) gives them a mouth. The soundscape of a place is who has and hasn't been recorded.
9. **Ungarnish: to strip ornament.** Creditors stripping a house while the owner is still breathing. As a verb: strip a decorated thing to its working structure, so you can see the mechanism you are standing on.
10. **Violableness and malleableize: rules that can be broken and matter that can be softened.** Precedent that binds until someone perjures. Rigid things made pliable by reclassification: a wall that becomes a curtain because the paperwork says so.

## 2. Five clichés to steer away from

1. **The hollow/decayed kingdom with a sad ambient soundtrack**, where the map is a ruin and the story is the ruin's autopsy (Hollow Knight, Blasphemous, Ender Lilies, most of the genre).
2. **The small silent creature in a big dead world** whose arc is "I was the cure / the key all along".
3. **Corruption spreading across the map** as the antagonist force: a colour that eats the world.
4. **Abilities as elemental powers** (fire dash, ice wall, lightning grapple) with the biome named after the element.
5. **Afterlife bureaucracy / purgatory waiting-room comedy** (Grim Fandango, Beetlejuice, Have a Nice Death, Death's Door). The mood seed says bureaucratic; that particular joke has been told.

## Concept A: PROBATE

**One-line hook:** You preside over the probate of a will whose testator refuses to die until every claim is witnessed, in a house so large and so contested that the only power you have is what you have on record.

### Premise and world logic

You are the Presider, a junior court officer sent to the Estate to convene probate *early*. The Testator, an enormous, breathing, bedridden presence in the central Bedchamber, is dying, but will not die: a testator cannot pass until the will is complete, and the will cannot be complete until every claim on the Estate has been witnessed and entered. The heirs, lovers, creditors, servants, and the animals and machines of the house have all lodged claims. Some of the claimants are people. Most are not.

The Estate is a single house that has been extended for generations by people who each wanted their own wing and never wanted to see each other: a Gormenghast of stairs, dumbwaiters, hoists, counting rooms, greenhouses and servants' passages inside the walls. Its timbers are old and the deathwatch beetles tick in them. At its edges, where nobody has bothered to survey, the house simply stops being on record: the Unfiled, an infinite void of unstained, silhouette-only space that you can walk into and fall through.

Why it is explorable like a metroidvania: the house obeys the Record. Doors, hoists, floors and claimants respond only to what has been witnessed and entered in the will. You are the only person allowed to enter things. So the map opens in proportion to what you have *seen*, not what you can *do*. Every area is reachable in principle from the start; you lack the information to make the house comply.

The deathwatch is the hub. You return to the Bedchamber to enter what you have witnessed. The Testator's breathing is the room's ambient sound (a slow guttural rattle, not music), and the will grows visibly on the wall as the game progresses. That's the save point, the map screen, and the story delivery in one place.

Tone: dry, adult, Dickens-by-way-of-Peake. Nobody in this house is good. Everybody wants something specific, and every one of those wants is on paper.

### Signature mechanic: Deposition and Citation

Two verbs, one system.

**Depose.** Hold the depose button while near any active thing (a claimant mid-attack, a hoist mid-climb, a chandelier mid-fall, a dog mid-leap) and you *witness* it. The thing's current action is captured as a short testimony: a strip of a few seconds of its motion and behaviour, stamped into your ledger in azophenine violet. A witnessed thing turns violet-edged in the world. Deposing takes time and leaves you open; you must be present, close, and looking while the thing does the thing. You cannot depose an idle object. It has to be *doing* something.

**Cite.** Point at any witnessed thing and press cite: it must repeat its testimony, now, regardless of its own state or intent. The hoist that you saw rise will rise, even though its counterweight is elsewhere. The heir you saw lunge will lunge, off a balcony if that is where she is standing. The dog will jump the gap it jumped before, and you can be on its back. Citation costs a **seal**; seals are the only resource, they replenish at the bedside and at notaries scattered through the house, and their count grows through the game.

Moment to moment, this makes the world a library of behaviours you have collected. You look at a room and see violet-edged things you can *make happen* and grey things you have not yet understood. A gap you cannot jump is not a "double jump needed" gap; it is a "you have not yet watched anything cross it" gap.

**In combat**, deposing is the risky move and citing is the payoff. Claimants (enemies) have two or three behaviours. Witnessing a behaviour means you have to survive it up close; once you have it, you can trigger it on demand. The heir's overhead swing, cited while she is under a low beam, makes her hit the beam. The creditor's grab, cited while he is next to another creditor, grabs the wrong man. A witnessed claimant can be forced into its own worst moment. There is also a plain weapon (the Presider's rod, a gavel on a long handle) so the player is never helpless, but the rod alone will not end a fight with anything that matters. Damage is not the win condition for claimants of significance: **a claimant is defeated when every one of its behaviours is on record**, at which point it is *fully witnessed*, its claim is entered, and it sits down. This is what "information is the reward" means here: you win encounters by learning them completely, and the same act of learning is what you take back to the will.

**Violableness.** Late in the game, claimants learn to perjure: they deviate from their testimony while being cited (they lunge, but shorter). Perjury is visible (the violet edge flickers and goes red-brown) and is itself a new behaviour that can be witnessed. The counter is not a new power; it is watching more carefully.

**How it gates.** Three gate types, all readable at a glance:
- **Not witnessed:** a grey thing that would help you if it were violet. Go find where it does the thing.
- **Not enough seals:** a sequence that needs three citations in a row when you can afford two.
- **Precedent locks:** doors and floors that open only to a Presider who has *entered* a specific class of testimony at the bedside ("the court has heard from the kitchens"). These are the traditional key gates, phrased as court process.

### Ability list

1. **The Rod** (start): a long gavel. Plain melee, can strike bells, knock props, tap timber to hear where the beetles are.
2. **Depose** (start): witness an active thing. The core.
3. **Cite** (start, 1 seal): compel a witnessed thing to repeat its testimony. Seal count grows from 1 to about 6.
4. **Continuance**: hold a cited action *paused* mid-testimony for as long as you hold the button. The hoist stops halfway. The heir stops mid-lunge and you walk around her. Reskinned "stasis", but only on things you have witnessed, which keeps it honest to the lens.
5. **Ghost of the Record**: citing a moving platform leaves a violet after-image of its path for a few seconds; the after-image is solid. This is the "air platform" ability, earned in the Counting House where hoists run on tallies you can't reach.
6. **Ungarnish**: strip a decorated thing (a panelled wall, a gilded machine, a costumed claimant) of its ornament, revealing its working structure. Mechanically: reveal hidden mechanisms, see through false walls, expose the behaviour cycle of a machine so it can be deposed at a distance. The visual is a silhouette resolve, ornament falling away as particles. Earned from the creditors' wing, where they have been doing exactly this to the furniture.
7. **Service**: throw a writ that sticks to any surface or claimant; you can be summoned to it once. A grapple with a filing metaphor and a paper trail: served writs remain on the map as visible notes.
8. **Adjournment** (late): cite *yourself*. You may replay your own last few seconds of movement as a violet ghost while you do something else. Used for two-switch problems and for one very specific boss.

### Biomes

1. **The Bedchamber and the Long Gallery** (hub). The Testator, the will on the wall, the family portraits (each portrait is a fast-travel node once its subject has been fully witnessed). Safe, slow, the rattle of the breathing.
2. **The Stacks** (archive). Paper, ladders, rolling shelves on rails. Everything here moves on a timetable and can be witnessed at leisure; the danger is falling shelving and the Archivist's clerks, who steal testimony out of your ledger (they un-witness things). This is where the player learns that the ledger can be attacked.
3. **The Interior** (inside the walls). Servants' passages, dumbwaiters, timber, beetles. Navigation by sound: tapping with the rod tells you where the wood is violable. Enemies are the house's own maintenance creatures. Tight, vertical, dark by silhouette rather than by lighting gimmick.
4. **The Counting House** (computative). The wing that runs the Estate's finances on mechanical calculators. Hoists, gates and locks are driven by running totals; you make them move by feeding numbers (carrying ledgers between machines) and by citing machines you have watched carry a figure. The deathwatch tick becomes a clockwork tick here. Bosses: the Comptroller.
5. **The Conservatory** (the lovers' wing). Overgrown, humid, the animals of the house. Ridable things: the dog, the peafowl, the hoist-monkey. The most open, most vertical biome, where citing living things is the traversal language. Also where the inheritance soap opera is loudest.
6. **The Unfiled** (the void). Beyond the last survey line. Grey, silhouette-only, no physics until it's on record: floors you cannot stand on until you've deposed something falling onto them. You bring the Record with you, and the world solidifies in violet behind you as you go. Late game. Visually the cheapest and most striking biome: everything is a procedural silhouette that resolves when filed.

### An encounter: the Eldest Daughter's hearing

A boss is a hearing. The Eldest Daughter has lodged the largest claim and she will argue it in the Long Gallery, a room of portraits and a great chandelier.

Phase one: she has three behaviours (the overhead cut, the lunge, the retreat-and-throw). You have to survive each at close range to depose it. The rod does trivial damage; you are there to watch.

Phase two: with all three on record she is *fully witnessed*, but she refuses to sit. She perjures: her lunge now stops short, her cut now feints. Each perjury is a fourth and fifth behaviour, and each is visibly marked. You must witness the perjuries too, which means eating a feint to learn it.

Phase three: the room is a claimant. The chandelier, deposed earlier in the game while it fell in another wing, can be cited above her; the portraits' subjects, if fully witnessed, step out of their frames and testify against her (each one you have completed lands a hit). She is defeated not when her health is gone but when the court has the whole of her on record, at which point she sits, and her claim is entered in the will, and the will gets longer.

What the player learns: bosses are people whose entire repertoire you must have seen. Preparation is exploration.

### Why it's not generic

Honest self-critique. The record-and-replay verb has relatives: the Talos Principle's recorders, Braid's shadow, Winterbottom, and closest of all the "observe the loop, then exploit it" reading of Outer Wilds and Tunic. The difference: those games make the *player* the holder of knowledge. Probate makes knowledge a resource *inside* the game with a count and a cost, and applies it to *other* things rather than to the player's own body. You never get stronger. The house gets more obedient.

Tonally, Gormenghast has never been a metroidvania, and inheritance drama has never been the plot of one. The absence of a kingdom, a corruption, a chosen one and an elemental ability set is the point.

Weakest part: "Continuance" and "Service" are stasis and grapple wearing wigs. Fine, but they will not carry the pitch. The signature verb has to.

### Greybox test

One room, one hour. Grey box, three things in it: a hoist that goes up on a fixed cycle, a spring trap that fires across a corridor, and one enemy that lunges and then rests. The player has: run, jump, rod, depose (hold near an *acting* thing), cite (press at a witnessed thing, costs one of two seals, seals refill on a timer).

Questions, in order:
1. Is *waiting to see the thing act* interesting, or is it just waiting? (If it is just waiting, ungarnish needs to move much earlier so the player can see cycles at a glance.)
2. Does citing the lunge into the spring trap feel like a plan, or like a gimmick you use once?
3. Does the hoist being citable feel better than the hoist being on a switch?

If two of three are yes, keep going.

### Risks

- **Pace.** Witnessing means standing near danger and doing nothing. If depose takes too long, combat is a tax. Tune depose to under a second and make it interruptible.
- **Onboarding.** "Win by watching, not by hitting" is counter-intuitive for the first hour. The rod has to feel good so players don't think the game is broken before they get it.
- **Combinatorics.** A library of behaviours is a QA surface: anything citable anywhere is a physics-exploit machine. Mitigate by scoping citation to the room (the ledger carries the testimony, but a thing must be present to be cited; you cannot summon the chandelier, you cite *this* chandelier).
- **Tone.** Dry adult humour is hard to get right and easy to make smug.

### Seeds used

- **deathwatch:** the hub is a vigil beside the dying Testator; the beetle in the timber is the Interior biome and the tap-to-hear-weakness mechanic.
- **presider:** the player's role and the entire verb set: depose, cite, adjourn, continuance. You preside; you do not conquer.
- **Sex, Death & the Infinite Void:** inheritance drama with lovers and heirs (the Conservatory), the death that hasn't happened, and the Unfiled as the void where the record stops.
- **azophenine:** the violet stain of everything on record; the single visual rule of the game.
- **computative:** the Counting House.
- **ungarnish:** the ability.
- **violableness:** perjury: testimony that can be broken, as the late-game escalation.
- **lens:** the only resource is information (testimony and seals), and encounters end when knowledge is complete, not when health is zero.
- **mood:** probate court, wills, writs, seals, notaries, precedent locks.

## Concept B: THE LANDING

**One-line hook:** On a customs pier jutting into an infinite void, nothing is real until it has been assessed, and you are the junior assessor with the only stamp, so a crate is whatever you say it is.

### Premise and world logic

The Landing is a port with no sea. It is a pier of warehouses, bonded stores, cranes and tariff halls built out from nothing into nothing: the Infinite Void, which delivers things. Nobody knows from where. Cargo drifts in from the grey, and until it has been assessed by the Board it is *in bond*: semi-real, flickering, unstable, without a fixed nature. An unassessed crate might be heavy or might float; it hasn't been decided yet. Assessment decides it. The Landing is the only solid place because it is the only place that has been fully assessed, and it grows outward as things are landed.

You are a Computative Assessor, Fourth Class. The Presider of the Board, the only person authorised to certify a full inventory, is dying (the deathwatch: Board members sit with him in shifts, and the meeting has not been adjourned in years because he cannot be replaced until the inventory is complete). Your job is to complete the inventory. That means assessing everything on the pier, including the things in the outer warehouses that nobody has been out to in living memory, and the things that have come in from the void and started, in the absence of a category, to behave like they resent it.

Why it's a metroidvania: the port is a single continuous structure of piers, gantries, bonded halls and hulls, and the tools you need are **categories**, which you learn by studying exemplars. Every category you learn is a new way to make the world behave. The far warehouses are full of things in bond that you cannot pass until you can tell the pier what they are.

Guttural: things in bond have no voice; they make throat-noises. Assessing a thing gives it a name and a mouth, and the assessed sections of the pier are the ones where you can hear speech. The void is silent. The outer warehouses growl.

### Signature mechanic: Assessment (the Stamp)

Every object in the world carries a **tag**, and the tag, not the object's appearance, decides its physics. There are a small number of categories, and each has a rule:

- **Fixture:** solid, immovable, standable. Default for the built pier.
- **Cargo:** heavy, pushable, falls, sinks in anything liquid, presses switches, crushes.
- **Correspondence:** light, rises slowly, drifts on air currents, can be ridden.
- **Vessel:** moves along the nearest painted lane at a steady rate, carries what stands on it.
- **Livestock:** animate, skittish, moves on its own, jumps, can be ridden with difficulty, flees from you.
- **Perishable:** exists for a countdown, then is gone. Anything blocking can be made temporary.
- **Malleable:** soft. Stretches between two anchors as a bridge or a trampoline; enemies made Malleable can be knocked flat and folded.

You hold the Stamp. You can **re-assess** any object in reach to any category you *know*, and you learn a category by **studying an exemplar**: finding the reference specimen for that category somewhere on the pier, standing with it, and filling in its form. The form is the pickup. Information is the reward: what you gain from exploration is the *definition of a kind of thing*, which is more powerful than any tool.

The bureaucratic limit is your **allocation**: you may hold only N re-assessments at once (start at 2, grow to about 7). Re-assessing an eighth thing reverts the first. So the world is a small number of things that you have made be something else, and you are always choosing which lies to keep.

Moment to moment: stamp a crate Correspondence and it lifts; ride it up; stamp it Cargo at the top and it drops onto the guard below; the guard, if you have Fixture, is now a step. Stamp the wall Malleable and bounce from it to the gantry. Stamp the gantry Vessel and it takes you along the painted lane through the shutter. Stamp the shutter Perishable and wait.

**In combat:** the things in bond that fight are fighting because they have no category and have started to make one up. They are, mechanically, objects with a bad temper. You do not have a weapon. You have the Stamp. A charging thing stamped Livestock bolts; stamped Cargo it drops like a stone mid-charge; stamped Malleable it can be flattened under a Cargo crate; stamped Fixture it becomes a wall you use against its friends. A fight is an argument about what the thing is, and you win it by being the one holding the paperwork. Formally assessing a hostile thing (stamping it with the category that *actually fits it*, which you learn by watching how it behaves before you stamp it) settles it: it stops fighting, gets a name, and goes into the inventory. This is the pacifist route, and it is also the completionist route, and it is the only way to get a boss's entry into the ledger.

**How it gates:** obstacles are things whose current category stops you. You need a category you don't yet have, or more allocation, or a way to get an exemplar out of a locked hall. Late gates involve **Appeals**: things whose tag has been *certified* by the Board and cannot be re-assessed until you've overturned the certification at a Board hearing, which is a boss.

### Ability list

1. **The Stamp** (start): re-assess a thing in reach to a known category. Two categories known at start: Fixture and Cargo.
2. **Correspondence** (form): things rise and drift. The "vertical movement" ability. Found in the Mail Hall, where the exemplar is a letter that has been rising toward the ceiling for a decade.
3. **Vessel** (form): moving platforms on demand, anywhere there's a painted lane. Found in the Dry Dock.
4. **Livestock** (form): things that move themselves. Mounts, decoys, self-solving switches.
5. **Malleable** (form): bridges, trampolines, and folding enemies flat.
6. **Perishable** (form): make anything temporary. The "break the wall" ability, done as a countdown you can watch.
7. **Self-Assessment** (late): stamp yourself. Correspondence on yourself is a glide; Cargo on yourself is a ground-pound and lets you sink through liquid; Malleable on yourself is a wall-bounce. This is the whole classic ability set delivered at once, gated by a single form that the Board does not want you to find.
8. **Allocation increases** (scattered): the "heart container" of this game, found by completing sections of inventory.

### Biomes

1. **The Tariff Hall** (hub). The Board sits with the dying Presider. The inventory is a great wall of pigeonholes that fills up as you go. Safe; talky; the only place with music-free speech from everyone.
2. **The Bonded Stores.** Vast stacked halls of things in bond. Everything flickers; everything is re-assessable; enemies are the more resentful crates. The tutorial for allocation: too many lies at once and your earliest ones revert under you.
3. **The Dry Dock.** Hulls of ships that came in without water. Painted lanes everywhere; the Vessel biome. Vertical, interlocking, and the first place the void is visible through the gaps in the planking.
4. **The Mail Hall.** Correspondence drifts on currents; a wind biome without wind, just things that rise. Sorting machines (computative) that route anything Correspondence along tracks: you can post yourself.
5. **The Pens.** Livestock that came out of the void and were never classified because nobody could agree what they were. Ridable, hostile, herd behaviour; the biome where combat is most about turning the animals on each other by re-tagging.
6. **The Outer Pier.** The last built section before the void, unlit, growling, where things in bond have been left so long they have invented their own categories that aren't on your form. This is where the game gets strange: things that are *Cargo while observed and Livestock otherwise*, things that are two categories at once. To assess them you have to file new forms, which means a trip back to the Board and an argument.

### An encounter: the Manifest

The Board certifies a thing on the Outer Pier called the Manifest: a great slab of a creature, tagged Fixture and certified, that blocks the last gantry. It cannot be re-assessed. The boss is an Appeal: you must prove to the Board that the certification is wrong, and the proof is *behaviour*. During the fight the Manifest does un-Fixture-like things (it moves, it spits Cargo, it grows Perishable limbs), and each time you witness a contradiction you gain a point of Appeal. The room is a Board hearing with the fight in the middle of it: clerks at the benches, the Presider's shift-sitters watching, and you dodging while filling in a form. Win enough Appeal and the certification is struck; the Manifest is now re-assessable and you stamp it, for one exquisite second, Perishable.

What the player learns: rules can be overturned, but only with evidence.

### Why it's not generic

Closest relatives: Baba Is You (rules decide behaviour; you rewrite them) and Scribblenauts (a noun makes a thing). Also Viewfinder, Superliminal and Control for the "the world is bureaucratically weird" tone. The difference from Baba: this is real-time, physical, per-object and scarce. You are not editing a rule sheet; you are holding a stamp with a limited allocation and pointing it at a crate that is about to fall on you. The difference from Control: no shooting, no powers, no corruption, and the Old House has a purpose here (it is a port) rather than being weird for its own sake.

Weakest part: seven categories with clean physics rules is a lot of physics to get right, and the pitch collapses if any of them feels mushy. Also, "the void delivers things" is one adjective away from "eldritch", and that would be a cliché in the making; the void has to stay boring and administrative.

### Greybox test

One room, one hour. A gap, a high ledge, a locked shutter with a floor switch behind it, three identical grey crates, one enemy that charges. Player has: run, jump, the Stamp with Fixture, Cargo and Correspondence known, allocation of 2.

Questions:
1. Is stamping-then-riding a crate up as fun as a double jump? (If the crate is faster to reach than the ledge is to think about, no.)
2. Does allocation feel like a choice, or like a nag? Does the third stamp reverting the first ever produce a good story?
3. Does stamping the charging enemy Cargo mid-charge feel like winning or like cheating?

### Risks

- **Physics scope.** Seven categories times every object type is a matrix. Keep categories to four for the prototype.
- **Puzzle-vs-action.** Stamping is a pointing action; if the game asks you to think while enemies are moving it can become fiddly. Enemies must be slow and legible.
- **The Baba comparison** will be made by everyone. It needs to be answered on screen in the first ten minutes with a moment Baba can't do: something falling on something because of the player's timing.
- **Tone drift into whimsy.** A customs house is funny; it must not be *cute*.

### Seeds used

- **presider:** the dying Presider of the Board whose replacement needs a complete inventory; the deathwatch shift-sitters; Appeals as boss fights.
- **deathwatch:** the reason the meeting never ends; the hub.
- **Infinite Void:** the sea that delivers cargo; the edge of the assessed world.
- **computative:** the assessor's title; the sorting engines of the Mail Hall; the inventory as tally.
- **malleableize:** a category, literally.
- **gutturality:** the voice of things in bond before they are named; the soundscape rule for assessed vs unassessed space.
- **lens:** the pickups are *definitions*; power is knowing what a thing is.
- **mood:** customs, bonds, tariffs, allocation, certification, appeal.

## Which is stronger

**Probate** is the stronger franchise. Its one visual rule (violet for what's on record, grey for what isn't) can be done entirely with silhouettes and shaders and reads at a glance; its verb pair (depose, cite) is rare in the genre and honours the lens exactly, because encounters end when your knowledge is complete rather than when a health bar is; and its setting, a Gormenghast inheritance, has no direct competitor. Its danger is pace: watching before acting can feel passive, and the greybox test is designed to find that out first.

**The Landing** has the better toy. Re-tagging a crate mid-air is a livelier moment-to-moment loop and might prototype faster. But it lives in Baba Is You's shadow, it carries a large physics matrix, and its voice is at more risk of sliding into whimsy.

If only one gets a greybox this week, Probate.
